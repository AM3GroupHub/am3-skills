# Practice-Talk Review Rubric (audio-based)

The criteria the skill applies when analyzing a transcribed practice talk. Distilled from the group guideline *How to make an impactful presentation* and the recurring feedback across many coaching sessions. **Cite the bracketed rubric tag** in each finding so the speaker can trace it back.

## Scope: what audio can and cannot reveal

A recording captures the **spoken talk**, not the slides. So this review judges **logic, structure, transitions, pacing, wording, and how results are explained out loud** — which is exactly what the three asks need. It does **not** see fonts, colors, figure layout, or word-count-per-slide. When the speaker also supplies the slide deck or title, fold it in as extra context; otherwise, infer slide structure from verbal cues ("on this slide…", "as you can see here…") and flag visual concerns only when the narration implies them.

Always work from **what was actually said**, with a short quote + timestamp for every finding. Never invent problems the audio doesn't support.

## Positive / mature-talk calibration

Some practice talks are already strong. In those cases, do not force a harsh critique just to fill the template. First name what the speaker should keep, then limit findings to the few refinements that would make the talk more conference-ready.

- **Preserve the mature spine.** A talk with a clear problem, motivated methods, a coherent benchmark/comparison, and a concise conclusion should be treated as mature. Critique only the places where a small change improves time, precision, or audience comprehension. `[positive: mature-spine]`
- **Use reviewer discussion as calibration, not raw delivery evidence.** If a transcript includes post-talk comments, separate the talk from Q&A and feedback before computing duration, filler, and sentence candidates. Reviewer comments can confirm priorities, but they are not part of the spoken talk. `[positive: separate-feedback]`
- **Polish instead of punish.** For strong talks, phrase remaining findings as polish tasks: trim, clarify, calibrate, or move to backup. Avoid overstating a minor issue as a structural failure. `[positive: proportionality]`

## Ask 1 — Logic gaps and structural problems

Detect where the argument is missing a step, out of order, or never lands.

- **Problem defined before method.** The single most common failure: methods/tools introduced before the physical problem and key concepts are defined. Listen for a method named (an algorithm, a model, an acronym) with no prior definition of the problem it solves or the terms it uses. → *Define the problem and every concept first, then the method.* `[logic: problem-before-method]`
- **A clear hook in the first ~30 s.** Does the opening pose a question, a striking result, or a real-world problem — or does it read the title / crawl through an outline? Reading the title back or opening with "today I will give an overview of…" is a miss. → *Open with the essence that grabs attention; never re-read the title.* `[logic: hook]`
- **One research question / take-home message.** Is there a single sentence the talk keeps returning to? If the talk is a tour of methods with no stated question ("what problem do you want to solve for the field?"), that's the core gap. → *State one research question early and repeat one take-home message.* `[logic: one-message]`
- **Motivate each method by the previous one's limit.** Each new method should answer a shortcoming just shown ("X fails because…, so we use Y"). Abrupt method-to-method jumps with no "why now" are gaps. → *Bridge methods with their motivation.* `[logic: method-motivation]`
- **Hypothesis before evidence.** Is the claim/hypothesis stated before the data meant to support it? Data dumped before the audience knows what it's for is a gap. `[logic: hypothesis-first]`
- **A real summary / conclusion.** Does the talk end on ≤3 distilled points, or trail into the acknowledgements? Missing or >3-point conclusions are a gap. → *End on ≤3 key points.* `[logic: summary]`
- **No unresolved threads.** A "weakness/caveat/we'll come back to this" that never resolves leaves the audience puzzling for a minute and losing the thread. → *Don't open a loop you won't close.* `[logic: open-loop]`
- **Move side questions to backup.** A relevant but noncentral caveat, extra method, or anticipated question can drain main-talk time if it is not needed for the core claim. → *Keep the main story lean; put adjacent explanations in backup slides or Q&A.* `[logic: backup]`

## Ask 2 — Smoothness, flow, and transitions

Detect where the delivery is choppy, rambling, or hard to follow.

- **Verbal signposts at transitions.** Listen for cues like "this raises the question…", "building on this…", "approach A works, but B is hard because…". Their absence (or "now something completely different") signals weak structure. → *Signpost every section change.* `[flow: signposts]`
- **Filler and disfluency.** Count and locate "um/uh/like/you know/so yeah", false starts, and self-corrections ("sorry, I mean…"). Cluster-heavy passages mark shaky, under-rehearsed spots. → *Rehearse the rough passages aloud until smooth.* `[flow: filler]`
- **Run-on / mouthful sentences.** Flag sentences that pile clauses without a breath or that the speaker stumbles through. → *Shorten; one idea per sentence; pause after key points.* `[flow: run-on]`
- **Command of own vocabulary.** If the speaker mixes up or fumbles their own core terms (e.g. swapping two key opposites), the audience has no chance. → *Drill the core vocabulary until reflexive.* `[flow: vocab]`
- **Pacing & timing.** Estimate duration and speaking rate (words ÷ minutes). Compare to the target slot (ask the user; common: ~10 min APS, ~12.5 min MRS/ACS). Over-target, or a rate >~160 wpm (rushing) or long dead pauses, are problems. Speaking faster does not buy time — cutting content does. → *Keep to the slot (~1 slide/min); slow down; pause deliberately.* `[flow: timing]`
- **Cut explanatory detours.** Mature talks often lose time in implementation justification: why a protocol choice was made, why a minor algorithm was used, or why a side limitation exists. If the explanation raises more questions than it answers, say what was done in one sentence and move on. `[flow: trim-detail]`
- **Internalized, not read.** Monotone, list-like, or recitation cadence suggests reading a script. → *Internalize the logic; speak it in your own words.* `[flow: internalize]`
- **Only what's understood.** Sections where the speaker hedges ("I didn't really prepare this", "I can't explain it in English") should be cut or deferred — you can't present what you haven't internalized. `[flow: prepared]`

## Ask 3 — Presenting results more effectively

Detect where findings are stated without being made understandable or compelling — judged from the narration.

- **Define the data before concluding.** When a figure/result is discussed, are the axes, quantities, symbols, units, and colors named *before* the conclusion? "As you can see, it's already identified" with no setup is the classic miss. → *Introduce axes, symbols, and what the data is, then conclude.* `[results: define-data]`
- **Conclusive framing, not topic labels.** Does the speaker state each result as a conclusion ("increasing X creates Y"), or just announce a topic ("now, the phase diagram")? → *Say the conclusion; (and on slides, make the title the conclusion).* `[results: conclusive]`
- **One result at a time.** Narration that races through many findings at once ("and here, and also here, and this too") overwhelms. → *Walk one key result through fully before the next.* `[results: one-at-a-time]`
- **Calibrated claims.** Flag over-strong claims ("X is wrong", "this proves") unsupported by the data, and vague claims ("the results are not that different", "it's reasonable") that dodge specifics. → *State a number or a bounded claim; don't overclaim or hand-wave.* `[results: calibrated]`
- **Caveats as facts, not excuses.** When explaining mismatch or limitations, separate known causes from speculation. "The quench rate differs from experiment" is stronger than a vague or unverified explanation such as "simulation scale" or "underrepresented data" unless the talk has evidence for that claim. `[results: caveat-calibration]`
- **Precise terminology.** Flag imprecise or overreaching terms — e.g. claiming "chemical accuracy" when "high accuracy" is meant, "AI-predicted" when "computationally predicted" is accurate, "weakness" where "caveat" fits. → *Use the precise word the field expects.* `[results: terminology]`
- **The "so what".** After a result, is its significance stated — why it matters, what it enables, who should care? Results presented without consequence fall flat. → *Follow each result with its significance.* `[results: so-what]`
- **Anticipate the obvious question.** Does the speaker pre-empt the predictable challenge (benchmark/accuracy/novelty vs. prior work), or leave it hanging? → *Address the likely question before it's asked (prepare a backup answer).* `[results: preempt]`

## Delivery & wording (supporting signals)

- **Don't read the title; don't repeat it** after being introduced. `[delivery: title]`
- **Define jargon and acronyms** on first use, especially for a non-specialist audience. `[delivery: jargon]`
- **Punctuality / Q&A time** — leaving time for questions; not running over. `[delivery: punctual]`
- **Keep acknowledgements efficient.** In a time-limited practice talk, thank collaborators and data providers briefly; leave routine funding, PI names, and recruiting details on the slide unless they matter to the venue. `[delivery: acknowledgements]`
- **Rehearsal** — audible under-preparation (heavy filler, fumbled terms, "I didn't prepare") → *rehearse the full talk ≥3× (first-time content: more); practice with AI/transcription as a check.* `[delivery: rehearse]`

## Sentence-level analysis for audio/video reviews

Use this section when the user provides audio/video or asks for sentence-by-sentence coaching. Focus on the most consequential sentences rather than every transcript line unless the user explicitly asks for a full pass.

- **Broken sentence.** Flag incomplete, self-repaired, or grammatically collapsed sentences that would make a formal audience lose the claim. → *Rewrite as one complete subject-verb-claim sentence.* `[sentence: broken]`
- **Mouthful sentence.** Flag sentences that carry too many clauses, definitions, and conclusions at once. → *Split into two or three short sentences; pause after the key claim.* `[sentence: mouthful]`
- **Ill-conditioned formal wording.** Flag vague or informal phrases ("stuff", "things", "kind of", "maybe", "you can see", "not really prepared", apologies) that weaken authority. → *Replace with precise, confident academic wording.* `[sentence: formal-tone]`
- **Broken local logic.** Flag sentences where evidence, comparison, variable definition, or consequence is missing. → *Use the chain: setup -> observation -> interpretation -> so what.* `[sentence: local-logic]`
- **Pronunciation / ASR clarity risk.** Flag repeated ASR fragmentation or mis-hearing of core technical terms as intelligibility risks, not accent problems. → *Drill the term slowly, then place it in the full sentence at talk speed.* `[sentence: pronunciation]`

## How to grade

For each ask, surface the **few highest-impact** findings (not every micro-issue). A finding = a short verbatim quote + ~timestamp, the problem, the concrete fix, and the principle tag. Lead with preserved strengths when the talk is strong, then structural/logic problems, flow, results framing, and sentence-level repairs. Close with the **top 3 priorities** and a one-line honest overall verdict (e.g. "strong content, under-rehearsed delivery", "clear delivery, but no stated research question", or "mature talk; final revision is mostly time trimming and claim calibration").
